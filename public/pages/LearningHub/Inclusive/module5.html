Training Objective

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify practical steps clubs can take from policy to programs to welcome people with disability.
  • Discover how to make club documents, social media, and events more inclusive.
  • Promote a culture where team players become inclusion champions.
  • Build a club plan or pledge that makes inclusion part of your everyday practice, not just a once-off.

“Inclusion doesn’t sit with one person.

Inclusion in the Boardroom

Inclusive sport doesn’t just happen on the field. It starts in the boardroom.

As leaders, committee members, and decision makers, you set the tone for your entire club. When you make inclusion a priority, you create a culture where people of all abilities feel welcome, safe, and valued.

The impact of that leadership ripples across every training, match day, and social event.

Flagstaff has collated key best practices designed especially for boards and club leadership teams to support people with disability, particularly those with cognitive and neurodiverse needs. These are practical, proven strategies that go beyond statements. They help clubs take real, meaningful action.

Foster a Positive Culture of Inclusion

  • Inclusion begins with attitude. Create a culture that celebrates difference and sees diversity as a strength.
  • Lead by example.
  • Make inclusion part of your mission statement and strategic plan.
  • Back up good intentions with real action, from accessible policies to inclusive programs.
  • Use simple phrases that build culture, like “Be kind. Be patient. Everyone belongs.”

Talk With, Not About, People with Disability

  • The most respectful and effective way to plan for inclusion is to involve the person and their family. Get local families involved in changing and adapting your culture to be more inclusive.
  • Ask: “What works well for you? What support would you like?”
  • Inclusion doesn’t mean creating separate spaces. It means adjusting so everyone can be part of the same team, in a way that works for them.

Be Flexible and Adaptive

  • Not every rule or drill works for every person. And that’s okay. Modifications will need to be made, and that is not a bad thing. It just takes some creative thinking on how to make it work for everyone.
  • Lead a culture of flexibility where your club adapts to the player’s needs, not the other way around.
  • Simple changes like using lighter balls or extra water breaks can make all the difference.

Use Clear and Accessible Communication

  • Avoid jargon and complex instructions. Speak clearly, calmly, and respectfully.
  • Use visual aids where possible, such as a printed schedule, a whiteboard with icons, or a simple checklist.
  • Ask the person what language or style they prefer. Put the person before the disability.

Establish Routines

  • Routines help players with autism, ADHD, and intellectual disability feel calm and confident.
  • Post the order of activities before training starts, such as warm-up, drill 1, game, and cool-down.
  • Predictability builds trust and reduces anxiety.

Build Sensory Friendly Environments

  • Consider loud sounds, flashing lights, or chaotic spaces. These can overwhelm some players.
  • Provide quiet areas or calm zones. Even a shaded pop-up tent with beanbags can make a big difference.
  • Allow headphones or fidget items if they help someone feel at ease.

Prioritise Safety and Wellbeing

  • Always have accessible First Aid available.
  • Know if a player has health needs, anxiety triggers, or emergency contacts.
  • Make sure gates are secure, pathways are clear, and helpers are briefed.
  • A buddy system or support person can help new players feel safe and settled.

How to Be Accessible

  • Support teammates with disabilities by understanding their needs.
  • Be patient and encouraging.
  • Help create an inclusive and welcoming environment.

How to Help Somebody with a Disability if They Are Upset

  • Preventing – Before supporting somebody with a disability, you should find out what things make them upset and happy and other information to help you understand more about the individual. Things that can upset people include loud noises, bright lights, big groups of people, and other stimuli that impact people’s senses.
  • Taking action – Just because somebody may look upset, does not always mean that they are, and vice versa. You should always ask somebody if they need help before taking action. You must also be patient when communicating as everybody communicates in their own way.
  • Communication – When communicating, always remember to focus on the person and not the disability. Don’t ask intrusive questions and avoid being judgmental, as you don’t know what people are going through. If you have any questions, keep them simple, stay calm, and don’t take anything somebody says about you personally when they are in distress.
  • Be an ally – Support and help your teammates who may need some help or guidance.
  • Use inclusive language – Put the person before the disability. For example, say “person with disability”, not “disabled person”. Say “person who has visual impairment”, not “blind person”.
  • Promote inclusion – Encourage everyone to join social events if there are any after sports, club meetings, or games.
  • Preparing for next time – Follow up with the person or somebody responsible to check in and take advice for what to do next time an incident occurs. Keep in mind the previous incident to make sure the same thing does not happen again. Take note of the things you can and cannot control in preparation for next time you see the person.
