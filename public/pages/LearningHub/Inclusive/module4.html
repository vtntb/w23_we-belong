Learning Hub > Inclusive Sports Training > Module 4 – From the Sideline – A Guide for Spectators

Training Objective

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Understand how spectators and volunteers shape the tone, safety, and welcome of a club.
  • Use positive, respectful language that encourages all players without placing pressure or blame.
  • Recognise and support individual sensory needs, including players who may need quiet or calm.
  • Foster family and community connection so that everyone on or off the field feels seen, safe, and included.

“The sideline should be a place of support, not stress. Everyone deserves to be cheered on with kindness.”

Creating a Respectful Sideline

Inclusion isn’t just something we do on the field, it lives in the tone, behaviour, and attitude of those around it. What we say and how we say it matters.

As someone in the crowd watching a sports game and players, consider that there are many types of abilities on the field or court.

Use Positive and Inclusive Language

  • Say things like “Great effort!”, “Well played!” or “You’re doing awesome!”
  • Avoid negative or demanding comments like “Try harder” or “What are you doing?”
  • Remember, even good intentions can cause harm. Some encouragement may feel overwhelming if the player can’t complete the task in that moment.

Be Mindful of Sensory Differences

  • Loud or unpredictable cheering, whistles, or music can be distressing for some players, especially those who are autistic or have sensory sensitivities.
  • Consider the environment. Clapping is better than shouting, and calmer praise may be more meaningful.

Keep the Focus on Support, Not Control

  • It’s natural to want to cheer and offer advice, but giving instructions like “Kick it now!” or “Run faster!” can cause pressure and confusion for some players.
  • Let the coach coach. Let the player play. You’re here to encourage, not direct.

Model Respect for Everyone

We model inclusion not just by cheering, but by the way we treat everyone involved.

  • Do not shout at or about referees, umpires, coaches or officials. Mistakes happen because they’re human too.
  • Never ridicule, swear at, or criticise any player, coach, official or spectator.
  • Comments that are racist, sexist, ableist, or discriminatory are never acceptable.
  • Be aware of body language and tone. How we look and react can speak just as loudly as words.

If you see behaviour that puts others at risk or undermines the club’s inclusive culture, let an official or coach know quietly and respectfully.

Spectator Tips for Inclusive Sidelines

  • Say positive things, or say nothing at all.
  • Cheer in a way that feels safe for everyone.
  • Let coaches coach, and players make their own decisions.
  • Avoid advice that might confuse or embarrass.
  • Never shout at referees, coaches, or players.
  • Report any unsafe or inappropriate behaviour to a club official.

Consistency for Spectators Too

  • Let spectators know the routine of the day, especially if families of people with disability are attending for the first time. A simple “What to expect” flyer or social story can help reduce anxiety.
  • Include clear signage at the venue, especially for entry, toilets, quiet areas, or accessible viewing spots.

Designated Viewing Areas

  • Some families or individuals may prefer a quieter space. Consider setting up an optional low-sensory zone near the field or court with shade, minimal crowd noise, and fewer distractions. This can make a big difference.
  • Ensure accessible seating for wheelchair users, older spectators, or those who may fatigue easily.

Inclusive Language in Announcements and Programs

  • If your club has printed materials or a PA system, use person-first and inclusive language when introducing teams or players. Put the person before the disability, or do not mention the disability at all. For example, say “player with disability” or just “team player”.
  • Encourage match day announcers to highlight participation and effort, not just competitive outcomes.

Welcoming New Spectators and Families

  • Consider assigning a club volunteer or buddy to greet new families, especially those attending with a child or adult with disability. A quick “Hi, we’re here to help if you need anything” goes a long way.
  • Offer a contact person if spectators have any questions about accessibility, inclusion, or safety during the game.

Include Families in Club Culture

  • Encourage families of players with disability to join in post-game activities, social events, and team photos, not just “watch from the car”.
  • Make sure social media and newsletters include photos and stories of all team members with consent. Representation matters.

Thank you for completing this module. We hope that you will encourage all spectators to be respectful and positive on the sideline.
